Observability Maturity

Nimbus has telemetry, but very little of it is usable during an incident. This page covers where we are, where we're going, and a practical order for getting there.

  • We're at Level 1 (Reactive): data exists, but names are inconsistent, dashboards are rare, and alerts are raw thresholds.
  • The biggest win is cheap: standard resource attributes on every service, and one golden-signals dashboard template applied to every feature.
  • Then move to SLOs and burn-rate alerts, starting with the two most-used interactive features.
Applies Anywhere

Most organizations start at Level 1. The order of the roadmap (standard attributes, then a dashboard template, then SLOs, then automation) works for any stack and any vendor.

For senior managers · where leadership matters most

Your Role

  • Make the roadmap a funded priority, not a side project for two Cloud Infrastructure engineers.
  • Require the shared standards from every CFT. Optional standards won't be adopted.
  • Set targets and review them monthly, for example 100% dashboard coverage for customer-facing features in 90 days.

Questions to Ask

  • What share of our customer-facing features have a dashboard and a reliability target?
  • How were our last five incidents detected: alerts or customers?
  • How long does it take to work out which team owns a problem?
  • What's blocking the next roadmap item?

The Business Case

  • Faster diagnosis means shorter outages and fewer affected users.
  • Less dependence on a few experts, and less on-call burnout.
  • Better capacity data means less AI spend wasted on over-provisioning.

Roadmap: From Under 10% to Standardized

The order matters. Each phase makes the next one cheaper.

Days 0–30: Foundations Quick Wins

  • Paved-road OTel package. Cloud Infrastructure ships a small shared library or config that sets resource attributes, the exporter, and sampling the same way for every service.
  • Standard attributes everywhere: service.name, service.version, feature.experience, cft.owner, deployment.environment.name.
  • Every alert has an owner. Delete or reroute alerts that nobody acts on.
  • A golden-signals dashboard template, provisioned as code with a $feature variable, so one template covers every feature.
  • A telemetry-volume alert, so silence is never mistaken for health.
  • Run at least two OpenTelemetry Collector replicas and alert on their dropped or refused spans. Every Rust service's telemetry goes through them.
  • Save the NOC shift sweep as shared queries. It's the NOC's dashboard until Grafana coverage catches up.
  • Client routing telemetry: the device orchestrator emits local vs cloud and fallback events, so fallback surges are visible.

Resource attributes vs customDimensions

The Rust opentelemetry crates read these standard environment variables and export OTLP to the Collector. The Collector's Azure Monitor exporter maps service.name to cloud_RoleName and service.version to application_Version. Custom resource attributes such as feature.experience may not reach customDimensions on every row. Copy them onto each span, either in the shared Rust telemetry crate or with a Collector processor (for example transform), so the queries in this guide can find them.

Days 31–60: Connect the Dots Medium Effort

  • Client trace propagation: the desktop client sends traceparent, and APIM forwards it.
  • GenAI semantic conventions on every Foundry call, including token usage and finish reasons.
  • A TTFT metric for every streaming feature, not only Writing Assist.
  • A Cloud Infrastructure saturation dashboard: tokens per minute against quota per deployment, PTU utilization, and 429s by caller.
  • First SLOs in shadow mode for Writing Assist and Device Care. Measure for 4 weeks before alerting on them.
  • Add P99 to latency queries and dashboards, so the slowest 5% that P95 hides becomes visible.
  • Runbooks for the top 5 alerts by frequency, each tested by a NOC engineer on a quiet shift.
  • NOC tooling for pre-agreed requests (scoped and audited), so the coordinator can act without a handoff.

Days 61–90: SLO-Driven Bigger Lift

  • Burn-rate alerts replace raw threshold alerts for features with SLOs.
  • Tail-based sampling in an OpenTelemetry Collector, keeping 100% of errors and slow traces.
  • An error budget policy agreed with CFT leads: what happens when the budget runs out.
  • Dashboard coverage: 100% of interactive features, all generated from the template.
  • First game day (throttle chat-small in staging).

Measuring Progress

  • Attribute coverage: the share of services with standard attributes (service inventory query).
  • Dashboard coverage: the share of feature experiences with a template-based dashboard. Today it's under 10%.
  • Alert ownership: the share of alerts with a named owner, and the share of pages acted on (versus ignored).
  • Time to scope: minutes from page to knowing the feature and owner, taken from incident timelines.
  • Detection source: the share of incidents detected by alerts rather than by users.
Careful with MTTR

Mean time to resolve is noisy and easy to game, and incidents vary too much for an average to mean much. Use it as a rough trend at most. Time to scope and detection source tell you more about observability maturity.
