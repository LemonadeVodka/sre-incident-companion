Incident Operations

How your organization runs an incident: who coordinates, who leads technically, how actions get requested and executed, and how the record is kept. You don't run these steps yourself. You make sure they happen, and you hold the vendor and the team to them.

  • The contract NOC is the Incident Coordinator (IC). An SRE is the Technical Lead. Owners (CFTs, Cloud Infrastructure, partners) execute.
  • The NOC has no production tooling, so it requests actions using pre-agreed requests, on timers.
  • You come in for decisions, unblocking, and executive communication (During an Incident).

The IC Loop

  1. What's the impact now?Ask the Technical Lead for the user-facing number, or re-run the sweep query yourself.
  2. What are we trying, and who's on it?Each workstream has one name next to it.
  3. Who haven't we heard from?Check the ladder timers for every paged team and every open action request.
  4. Is any decision waiting?Get it made: by the Tech Lead (technical), the owner (their system), or the senior manager (business).
  5. Update.Post the Slack update, mirror it to Teams through the bridge, and update the pinned record.

Running Across Slack and Teams

  • Decisions only count in Slack. Anything agreed in a Teams call gets posted in Slack, prefixed with [from Teams].
  • Join the Teams call if a Teams-only CFT is doing most of the work, and keep a bridge person on Slack.
  • Use the Slack workflows for declaring and the timeline. They don't exist in Teams.
  • Templates: updates and the Teams mirror.

Handling Executive Interruptions

Leaders under pressure sometimes dive into the details: they ask for raw queries, message responders directly, open side calls, or ask for root cause while users are still affected. (At Nimbus, see Morgan in The People.) Each interruption costs minutes. Handling this politely and firmly is part of coordinating.

  1. Name a comms buffer early.For Sev 1 it's the senior manager. For Sev 2 it's you. Announce it: "Questions about status go to @buffer, so responders can focus."
  2. Redirect, don't ignore.Acknowledge the question, point to the next update, and make sure it gets answered there.
  3. Pull side calls back.If a separate call or Teams thread starts, ask that its decisions be posted in Slack, and invite the starter into the main channel.
  4. Give them a job.Executives are most useful making decisions: spend, priorities, contacting other leaders.
  5. Escalate kindly.If the interruptions continue, ask the senior manager to step in privately.

Closing the Incident

  • The Technical Lead confirms impact has been at baseline for 30 minutes. Post the resolution update in Slack, mirror it to Teams, and resolve in PagerDuty.
  • Temporary changes still in effect have an owner and an undo time.
  • File the Jira ticket within 24 hours, using the pinned record (how).
  • Schedule the postmortem before standing down (required for Sev 1–2), and invite every CFT involved, Teams users included. Use the postmortem process.
  • Hand the timeline to the postmortem author.
  • Assign a runbook owner if no playbook fit.

How to Ask

  1. Name the owner.Page their PagerDuty service, or @ the named on-call. Never say "anyone".
  2. Name the action.Use the action names below where you can, so there's no ambiguity.
  3. Give the reason in one line, with the impact numbers.
  4. Set a check-back time, and start the ladder timer.
  5. Record it in the pinned record: requested at, acknowledged at, done at.

Pre-Agreed Requests

The owners below have agreed to perform these on NOC request within 15 minutes for Sev 1–2, without needing a long discussion. They're low risk and easy to reverse.

Pause the Next Step Background Batch

Executes: Insights CFTRisk: low (tips arrive late)
Request when
429s or latency on chat-small are hurting Device Care, and the batch is running.
They do
Set the next_step.batch_enabled flag (Nimbus-specific) to false.
Undo
Flag back to true. The batch resumes from its queue.

Halt a Desktop Client or Local Model Rollout

Executes: Client CFT / release managerRisk: low (delays a release)
Request when
Errors, 401s, crashes, or local-to-cloud fallbacks are concentrated in the version that's ramping, including on-device model updates.
They do
Pause the ramp in the release dashboard. Users who already updated keep the new version.
Undo
Resume the ramp once a fix is confirmed.

Restart One Unhealthy Service Instance

Executes: owning CFT or Cloud InfrastructureRisk: low, one at a time
Request when
One instance is much slower or failing health checks (a likely Rust async runtime stall), and its peers are healthy.
They do
Restart that one instance. Keep the instance name for the investigation.
Undo
None needed. If all instances look bad, it's not an instance problem.

Scale Out a Service by a Small Step

Executes: owning CFT or Cloud InfrastructureRisk: low (cost)
Request when
Every instance is CPU- or queue-saturated and dependencies look healthy. Not for 429s, because more instances mean more model calls.
They do
Add up to 50% more instances, within the service's documented maximum.
Undo
Return to the previous count after the incident.

Turn on the In-App "Degraded Service" Banner

Executes: Client CFT (server-driven config)Approval: senior manager for custom text
Request when
Sev 1–2 is declared and users are visibly affected.
They do
Enable service_banner with the approved "Some features are slower than usual" text.
Undo
Disable when the incident is resolved.

Future: NOC-Executable Actions

Once the NOC has tooling (scoped permissions, audited buttons, or runbook automation), the pre-agreed requests above are the first candidates to become things the NOC can do itself. Until then, every request costs a handoff and a wait. Tracking how long requests take is the best argument for the tooling. See the manager asks.

  • Measure request-to-done time for each pre-agreed request (from pinned records).
  • Agree which actions the NOC may run directly, with owners and security.
  • Build scoped, audited tooling for them (for example, a runbook automation job with a confirmation step).
  • Move each action from "request" to "NOC executes", one at a time, after a game day.

Recommending Mitigations

You don't execute changes, and neither does the NOC. Owners do. Make it easy for them to say yes:

TextMitigation RecommendationTo the IC, who turns it into an action request
Recommend: [action] by [owner team]
Why: [evidence — query link + numbers]
Expected effect: [..] within ~[N] min
Risk: [low/medium — what could go wrong]
Undo: [how]
If declined: [next best option]
  • Prefer reversible actions: pause, halt, shift, or degrade before you redeploy.
  • One change at a time, so you can tell what worked.
  • Decisions that cost money or quality go to the senior manager through the IC.

Protecting Focus

A leader DMs you: "What query are you running? Why isn't this fixed?" Every answer costs minutes and your train of thought, and there's usually a follow-up. (At Nimbus, see Morgan in The People.)

  • Don't answer DMs mid-investigation. Reply once and move on: "I'm heads-down; @IC has the latest and I'll update them at HH:MM."
  • Share one chart, not raw queries. When leaders need to see something, post a single chart in the channel through the IC.
  • Don't debate hypotheses with spectators. Write them in the channel. Anyone with evidence can add to them.
  • Ask the IC for cover. "@IC can you take questions for a while? I need 15 minutes." That's a normal request.

"I'm heads-down on the fix. @IC has the latest status and I'll brief them at 14:30. Thanks for understanding."
