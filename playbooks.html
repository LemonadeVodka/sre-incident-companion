Starter Playbooks

We're a new team without many runbooks yet. These ten starter playbooks cover the incidents we expect most. They aren't battle-tested: improve them after every incident, and copy one as the starting point for a new runbook.

  • Every playbook has the same shape: Symptoms → Check First → Likely Causes → Verify, then NOC Coordinates → SRE Leads → Manager Lens, then Weak / Good / Best and Postmortem Focus.
  • Not sure which applies? Use the triage tool. No match? Follow the no-runbook method and write a new one afterwards.
  • Managers: the same scenarios are in plain English in Scenarios.
Applies Anywhere

The playbook shape works for any service: symptoms, checks, causes, verify, who does what, what good looks like, and what to ask in the postmortem. Swap the Nimbus specifics for your own.

Make Them Yours

After each incident, update the playbook you used: what was missing, what was wrong, what worked. Use the runbook template for incidents that didn't fit any playbook.

Latency Spike on One Feature

Usually Sev 2–3Owner: Feature CFTStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • P50 and/or P95 for one feature (say, Writing Assist) well above baseline. Other features normal.
  • Users report that one action is slow.
Check first
  1. Latency by feature, now vs yesterday: confirm only one feature is affected.
  2. P50/P95 per hop: model, retrieval, or our own code?
  3. Errors by service version: did a deploy line up with the start?
  4. Token growth: did prompts get bigger?
Likely causes
  • A service deploy, or a prompt template change that added context.
  • A Rust service stalling (async runtime starvation, pool exhaustion). Check by instance.
  • A retrieval dependency (AI Search, Graph) slowed down.
  • The feature's deployment is saturated.
Verify
P50 and P95 back within 20% of baseline for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Declare and coordinate. Page the CFT, plus SRE for Sev 2. If one instance looks stalled, send the restart-one-instance request to its owner.
Your SREs should
Split latency by hop and by instance. Roll back the deploy or prompt flag if it lines up. Tighten the retrieval timeout and degrade gracefully.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Did something change, and can we roll it back?" Decide: approve the rollback even if it delays a launch.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

Waits for a dashboard that doesn't exist, debugs in DMs, and nobody owns the rollback decision.

Good

Confirms with the per-feature query, declares, and the Tech Lead finds the hop. The owner rolls back within 30 minutes.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus the change that caused it is identified, the feature gets a latency SLO, and a deploy check is added.

Postmortem focus
Why didn't our deploy process catch the latency cost? Is there a dashboard for this feature now? Start the postmortem →

429 Throttling Surge

Sev 2 if interactiveOwner: Cloud Infrastructure + noisy CFTStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • Spike in 429 on Foundry dependencies.
  • "Nimbus is busy, try again." Latency also rises because of retries.
Check first
  1. 429 by deployment and caller
  2. Token consumption by caller: who is using the quota?
  3. Retry detector: is it a retry storm?
  4. Azure Monitor: the deployment's PTU utilization and its tokens-per-minute limit.
Likely causes
  • A background burst (Next Step batch) on a shared deployment.
  • An organic peak above capacity.
  • Prompt growth.
  • Retries without backoff, or unbounded concurrency in a Rust service.
Verify
Throttle rate under 1% for interactive callers for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Declare and coordinate. Page Cloud Infrastructure plus SRE. If Next Step is running and Device Care is affected, send the pause-batch request to the Insights CFT. Never request a scale-out for 429s.
Your SREs should
Prove who the noisy neighbor is, then shed background load, enable spillover with Cloud Infrastructure, and check retry and concurrency settings.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Whose traffic, can it pause, and what does overflow cost?" Decide: which feature gets priority, and whether to approve the overflow spend.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

Teams argue about whose traffic it is while users get errors, and retries make it worse.

Good

The noisy neighbor is proven with tokens by caller, background load is paused on request, and spillover is approved.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus interactive and background traffic get separate capacity, and an alert on quota share fires before the 429s do.

Postmortem focus
Who decides capacity priority between CFTs? Why was background work sharing interactive capacity? Start the postmortem →

Foundry 5xx / Platform Degradation

Sev 1–2Owner: Cloud InfrastructureStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • 500/502/503 on Foundry dependencies across features.
  • Model latency up across deployments, sometimes in one region.
Check first
  1. Model latency and failures by deployment, grouped by target (region).
  2. Azure status, Service Health, Foundry resource health.
  3. Errors by layer: confirm it's the model layer, not ours.
Likely causes
  • An Azure platform incident in the region.
  • A provider-side model version problem.
  • Network or private endpoint problems.
Verify
Error rate back to baseline. Track failback as its own change.
Your NOC should
Declare at high urgency, page Cloud Infrastructure plus SRE, and send the in-app banner request to the Client CFT for Sev 1–2. Open the exec comms buffer early.
Your SREs should
Confirm scope by region and deployment. Coordinate failover with Cloud Infrastructure. Open an Azure support case early. Shed background load.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Is it Microsoft's side, is a case open, can we fail over?" Decide: approve failover (data residency, cost), executive notice, and customer messaging.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

Everyone waits on Microsoft and nobody tells users.

Good

The scope is confirmed, an Azure case is opened early, failover is evaluated, and the banner is turned on.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus failover was tested in a game day, and there's a pre-approved decision tree for the senior manager.

Postmortem focus
How long did failover take compared with our target? What did we depend on that we couldn't control? Start the postmortem →

Slow First Token

Sev 2–3Owner: Feature CFT → Cloud InfrastructureStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • Streaming features feel slow to start, even when total time looks normal.
Check first
  1. Time to first token vs total
  2. Input token growth
  3. PTU utilization: is traffic queueing or spilling to pay-as-you-go?
  4. Is streaming still on? A config change or APIM buffering can turn it off.
Likely causes
  • PTU saturation and queueing.
  • Prompt or context growth.
  • Retrieval added before the model call.
  • Streaming disabled or buffered.
Verify
Writing Assist first-token P95 back under 1.5 s for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Coordinate and page the feature CFT. Ask the Tech Lead whether other features on the same deployment are affected, and page Cloud Infrastructure if so.
Your SREs should
Separate capacity from prompt-size causes. Shed background load, trim context or revert the prompt, and restore streaming.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Capacity or how we build the request?" Decide: accept a temporary quality trade-off (smaller model, less context) for speed.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

Treated as a vague 'Nimbus is slow' complaint and closed without a measurement.

Good

First-token time is measured, the cause is split between capacity and prompt size, and the prompt or capacity is fixed.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus first-token time is an SLO for every streaming feature, and prompt changes are tested for latency before release.

Postmortem focus
Do we measure time to first token for every streaming feature? Start the postmortem →

Errors After a Desktop Client Rollout

Sev 2 while rampingOwner: Client CFTStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • Errors, 401s, or crashes concentrated in the newest client version.
  • Support tickets saying "since the update".
Check first
  1. By client version: compare the new build with the previous one.
  2. The rollout percentage and how fast it's ramping.
  3. What changed in the build: API version, retry or timeout config, token refresh, telemetry.
Likely causes
  • A new build calls a changed API.
  • Aggressive retries.
  • A token refresh bug, which shows up as 401s about an hour in.
  • A Rust panic on an unexpected response.
Verify
The new version's error rate matches the previous version's.
Your NOC should
Send the halt-rollout request to the Client CFT straight away, then declare and page SRE.
Your SREs should
Confirm it's the cohort. Use a server-side flag to disable the new code path if one exists. Make sure the rollout stays paused until a fix is verified.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Has the rollout been halted? What share of users have the new version?" Decide: hold the release, and coordinate messaging.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

The rollout keeps ramping while people debate.

Good

The halt is requested within minutes, the cohort is confirmed, and the rollout stays paused until a fix is verified.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus rollouts halt automatically on error, crash, 401, or fallback thresholds.

Postmortem focus
Why didn't the staged rollout stop itself? Start the postmortem →

Dashboards Green, Users Complaining

Sev 3 until measuredOwner: SRE → Cloud InfrastructureStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • Support tickets or social posts say Nimbus is broken. Signals look normal, or quiet.
Check first
  1. Telemetry volume: did data drop? Is the OTel Collector healthy?
  2. Edge errors: a failure before our code runs won't show in App Insights.
  3. By client version and by region.
  4. Get 3–5 concrete user reports (time, feature, version) and look for their traces.
Likely causes
  • Client-side failures (TLS, proxy, clock, crash).
  • The Collector dropping data.
  • A feature with no instrumentation.
  • Quality problems: responses succeed but are wrong.
Verify
You have a metric that shows the problem and its recovery. Add it to a dashboard as a follow-up.
Your NOC should
Take user reports seriously. Declare at Sev 3, page SRE, and collect 3–5 concrete reports (time, feature, version).
Your SREs should
Get a measurement first (an ad-hoc Grafana panel), then follow the evidence. File the monitoring gap.
You ask & decide
Ask: "How do we know how many users are affected?" Decide: treat "customers told us first" as a serious finding, and fund the monitoring fix.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

"Dashboards are green", so the ticket is closed.

Good

Concrete reports are collected, telemetry volume and edge logs are checked, and a measurement is built.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus the blind spot is fixed (an alert on telemetry volume, client-side metrics) and the gap is tracked to closure.

Postmortem focus
Why did customers tell us before our monitoring did? Start the postmortem →

CDN / Front Door Errors

Often Sev 1–2 (broad)Owner: Cloud InfrastructureStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • 502/503/504 at the edge, often across every feature at once.
  • Or 403s for a group of users (WAF).
  • App Insights may look quiet, because requests never reach our services.
Check first
  1. Edge status codes over time.
  2. Edge errors by error info and route: OriginConnectionError, OriginTimeout, health probes?
  3. WAF blocks by rule if you're seeing 403s.
  4. Who changed what: Front Door, WAF, DNS, certificates.
  5. Azure status for Front Door. Is it one POP or region?
Likely causes
  • APIM unreachable or unhealthy (origin connection, private link, DNS).
  • Origin too slow, so timeouts. Often model latency underneath.
  • Health probes failing, so every origin is marked down.
  • A new or changed WAF rule blocking real traffic.
  • An expired or misconfigured origin certificate.
  • An Azure edge incident.
Verify
Edge 5xx under 0.1% and 403s back to baseline for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Declare at high urgency and page Cloud Infrastructure plus SRE. Purging the cache and changing WAF rules are Cloud Infrastructure decisions, so don't request them without the Tech Lead.
Your SREs should
Use the layer query to separate edge from origin. Work with Cloud Infrastructure on origin health, reverting the WAF rule, or rerouting. Communicate early, because impact is usually broad.
You ask & decide
Ask: "All users or some regions? Did anyone change a CDN or firewall setting?" Decide: approve the WAF rollback (with security sign-off) and customer messaging.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

"Our services look fine", so the problem bounces between teams.

Good

The layer query proves it's the edge, Cloud Infrastructure gets the evidence, and the WAF rule is reverted or traffic rerouted.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus WAF and CDN changes go through canary rollouts, with alerts on edge 5xx and 403.

Postmortem focus
How are changes to shared edge configuration reviewed and announced? Start the postmortem →

401 Surge

Sev 1–2 if widespreadOwner: Cloud Infrastructure / Client CFTStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • 401s rising at APIM, or on our services' calls to Foundry.
  • "Please sign in again" loops, or features refusing to work.
Check first
  1. 401s at the gateway by API: every API, or one?
  2. 401s by client version: one build, or everyone?
  3. 401/403 from our services to dependencies: managed identity?
  4. Token validation errors: expired, not yet valid (clock skew), or wrong audience?
  5. Who changed what: APIM policies, app registrations, secrets, role assignments.
Likely causes
  • APIM validate-jwt policy change (audience, issuer).
  • Entra ID incident or signing key rollover.
  • An expired client secret or certificate.
  • A token refresh bug in a new client build.
  • A managed identity role assignment removed (401 or 403 to Foundry).
  • Device clock skew (a small, scattered cohort).
Verify
401 rate back under 0.3% at APIM, and no auth failures on dependencies, for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Declare and coordinate. One client version: send the halt-rollout request and page the Client CFT plus SRE. All versions: page Cloud Infrastructure plus SRE.
Your SREs should
Find the rejecting layer, then the cohort, then the change. Revert the policy or config, or renew the secret with Cloud Infrastructure. Watch for a token refresh stampede during recovery.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Everyone or one app version? What changed in sign-in, keys, or certificates?" Decide: approve emergency sign-in changes, and ask for a secret-expiry calendar afterwards.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

Users are told to sign in again while secrets quietly expire.

Good

The rejecting layer, cohort, and change are found, and the policy is reverted or the secret renewed.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus there's a secret and certificate expiry calendar with alerts, and auth policy changes are canaried.

Postmortem focus
Did we know this secret or policy was about to change or expire? Start the postmortem →

Local-to-Cloud Fallback Surge

Sev 2, Sev 1 if broadOwner: Client CFT + Cloud InfrastructureStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • Cloud requests, tokens per minute, and 429s climb with no server-side deploy.
  • The rise follows a client or on-device model rollout curve, not the time of day.
  • Quick tasks feel slower for users on the new version.
Check first
  1. Fallback rate and reasons by local model version.
  2. Local vs cloud split by client version.
  3. Tokens per minute by caller: which features are absorbing the extra load?
  4. Release dashboard: what client or local model version is ramping?
Likely causes
  • A buggy on-device model update.
  • An NPU or GPU driver update breaking local inference.
  • Memory pressure on devices causing local attempts to fail.
  • A routing policy change sending more work to the cloud.
Verify
Fallback rate and local share back to baseline. Cloud 429s under 1% for 30 minutes.
Your NOC should
Declare and coordinate. Send the halt-rollout request to the Client CFT, page Cloud Infrastructure and SRE, and get both teams into one channel.
Your SREs should
Correlate the fallback rate with the rollout. Recommend halting the rollout (Client CFT) and adding capacity or shedding background load (Cloud Infrastructure). Keep both teams on the same call.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Did a device or model update go out, and are both teams on it?" Decide: hold the device release, and approve temporary cloud spend.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

The cloud teams fight 429s while nobody looks at devices.

Good

The fallback rate is correlated with the rollout, the rollout is halted, and capacity is added. Both teams work together.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus the fallback rate is a rollout health gate, and the cloud caps fallback traffic per version.

Postmortem focus
Why wasn't fallback rate a release gate? Start the postmortem →

Partner Integration Degraded

Sev 2–3 (one feature, one partner)Owner: Partner Integrations CFTStarter · improve after use
Symptoms
  • One feature fails or slows only for users of one partner (for example Catch Up with Zoom).
  • Partner dependency latency or errors rise. Other partners and features are normal.
  • Partner 401s (expired OAuth or consent) or 429s (partner rate limits).
Check first
  1. P50/P95 and errors by partner: one partner or all?
  2. Partner 401/429 breakdown: auth, limits, or partner outage?
  3. The partner's status page and any partner notices (API changes, deprecations).
  4. Who changed what and our connector deploys: did we change the connector?
Likely causes
  • A partner outage or degradation.
  • Partner API or schema change breaking our connector.
  • OAuth token refresh or consent expiry (401).
  • Hitting partner rate limits (429), often from retries.
  • Webhook delays on the partner side.
Verify
Partner dependency errors and latency back to baseline, or the connector cleanly disabled with a user-facing fallback.
Your NOC should
Declare and coordinate. Check the partner's status page, notify the partner contact, and page the Partner Integrations CFT plus SRE. If the partner is down, request the per-partner kill switch from the Partner Integrations CFT.
Your SREs should
Separate partner-side from connector-side. Recommend disabling just that connector with a graceful fallback. Check that timeouts and the circuit breaker kept the rest of the feature healthy.
You ask & decide
Ask: "Partner or us? Who owns the partner relationship right now?" Decide: disable the integration, approve messaging that names the partner, and escalate through the partner's account or executive contact.
Weak / Good / Best
Weak

We debug our own code for an hour while the partner's status page shows an outage.

Good

The partner layer is located quickly, the connector disabled with a fallback, and the partner engaged through their escalation path.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus per-partner circuit breakers, synthetic checks against each partner, SLA tracking, and a quarterly partner review.

Postmortem focus
Did our design contain the partner failure, or did it spread? Is the partner meeting its SLA? Start the postmortem →
