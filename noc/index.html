NOC: First Line of Defense and Incident Coordinator

You usually see a problem first, and once an incident is declared, you run it. The NOC detects, declares, and coordinates: channel, roles, cadence, communication, escalation timers, and the timeline. You don't have tooling to change production yet, so you get things done by requesting actions from the people who own them.

  • Detect: run the shift health sweep. Most services have no dashboard, so the sweep is your dashboard.
  • Declare and coordinate: you're the Incident Coordinator (IC) for every declared incident. SRE is the Technical Lead.
  • Request, don't act: ask owners for specific actions using the pre-agreed requests, and chase them on timers.
  • Close the loop: schedule the postmortem and hand over your timeline. Postmortems are one of our weakest spots.
Applies Anywhere

Separating coordination from technical investigation is the single most effective incident practice. The coordinator keeps the big picture, the cadence, and the people. The technical lead keeps their head in the problem. Neither tries to do both.

Reality Check: Coordinating Without Tooling

You can see almost everything and change almost nothing. That's fine for a coordinator, as long as requests are specific, timed, and recorded: "Insights CFT, please pause the Proactive Tips batch now. I'll check again at 14:30." See Requesting Actions. Getting the NOC executable tooling is on the manager asks list.

When to Bring in SRE

Page SRE on-call (PagerDuty "Nimbus SRE") as Technical Lead when any of these is true:

  • User impact is confirmed and above the baselines for more than 10 minutes.
  • More than one feature is affected, or the problem is at a shared layer (Front Door, APIM, auth, cloud orchestrator, Foundry).
  • After 15 minutes you still can't tell which layer or owner is involved.
  • A CFT hasn't acknowledged within 15 minutes, and you need someone to judge the technical options.

When SRE joins, you stay IC. Hand them the technical picture (template on Triage & Escalate) and keep coordinating.

Shift Rhythm

Start of Shift

  • Read the previous shift's handoff note and any open #inc- channels. You may be inheriting IC.
  • Check PagerDuty shows you on call, and that your phone gets a test notification.
  • Run the shift health sweep.
  • Check scheduled changes: client rollouts, local model updates, CFT deploys, Cloud Infrastructure changes, Azure maintenance.
  • Open the Teams channels for the Collab and Device CFTs, so you can bridge quickly.

During the Shift

  • Re-run the sweep every 2 hours, and after any rollout or change you hear about.
  • Acknowledge every page within 5 minutes.
  • Keep notes as you go. They become the handoff and the postmortem timeline.

End of Shift

  • Open incidents: status, IC handover (template on Coordinate), next update time.
  • Outstanding action requests: who owns each one, and when you're due to check.
  • Postmortems to schedule, and timelines to hand over.
  • Noisy or confusing alerts, for the runbook backlog.

What Good Looks Like

Weak / Good / Best in Class: NOC as Incident Coordinator
Weak

Notices late, debugs instead of coordinating, pages in chat, nobody knows who's in charge, and the timeline is rebuilt from memory.

Good

Declares within 10 minutes, runs a clear channel with roles and a cadence, pages through PagerDuty with timers, and makes specific action requests.

Best in Class

Everything in Good, plus executive questions are absorbed before they reach responders, the timeline is postmortem-ready at close, and the review is scheduled before standing down.
