Incident Process

How we run incidents across the NOC, SRE on-call, five CFTs, a Cloud Infrastructure CFT, and senior management, from the first alert to the last action item. For how this works with our actual tools (PagerDuty, Slack and Teams, a locked-down Jira), see Ways of working.

  • Six phases: detect, triage, declare, mitigate, resolve, learn.
  • Severity follows user impact. If you're unsure, go higher. Sev 1–2 always get an Incident Coordinator (the NOC) and a Technical Lead (SRE).
  • The affected CFT owns the incident until the evidence points to shared infrastructure. Mitigate before you debug.
Applies Anywhere

Whatever your tools, the same lifecycle applies: detect, triage, declare, mitigate, resolve, learn. Separating coordination from technical investigation is the most valuable habit to build.

For senior managers · where you fit in the response

Your Role

  • You're usually not the IC. You're the escalation point for decisions and resources.
  • Handle executive and customer messaging through the comms lead.
  • Approve costly or risky mitigations quickly.

Questions to Ask

  • What severity is this, and why?
  • Is there one channel and one IC, even with several teams involved?
  • Should customers or partners hear from us, and when?
  • Is there a decision waiting on me?

Avoid

  • Downgrading severity to make the numbers look better.
  • Starting a second channel or bridge for executives that pulls responders away.

When in doubt, go higher

Downgrading a Sev 2 to a Sev 3 costs one message. Under-declaring costs hours of lost coordination. Severity can change at any time, and the IC owns that call.

Roles

These roles come from the Incident Command System that firefighters use, which Google's SRE book adapted for software. At Nimbus the NOC is the Incident Coordinator, the SRE on-call is the Technical Lead, and owners (CFTs and Cloud Infrastructure) make the changes. In a small incident one person may hold several roles, but coordinating and debugging should never be done by the same person for long.

Incident Coordinator (IC): NOC

Owns the incident's coordination: declares, sets the cadence, assigns roles, runs escalation timers, and keeps the record. Doesn't debug or change production.

Technical Lead: SRE

Leads the investigation, finds the failing layer, and recommends mitigations with evidence. Owners execute.

Comms Buffer

Fields executive and stakeholder questions so responders can work: the senior manager for Sev 1, the IC for Sev 2.

Scribe

Keeps the timeline: what was observed, decided, and changed, with timestamps. Writing the postmortem is much easier with a good timeline.

Subject-Matter Experts

CFT engineers and Cloud Infrastructure specialists pulled in by the IC for a specific question.

Coordinating Across CFTs

  1. The NOC coordinates. The affected CFT owns the fix.If one feature experience is affected, that CFT owns the technical fix and SRE leads the investigation.
  2. Pull in Cloud Infrastructure on evidence, not hunches.Signs it's Cloud Infrastructure's problem: several features affected, model dependency latency or errors rising across deployments, 429s on a shared deployment, or gateway errors.
  3. One incident, one channel.When several CFTs notice the same problem, the NOC merges them into one incident with one coordinator. Parallel investigations waste effort and send mixed messages.
  4. Hand off out loud.When IC moves between NOC shifts, or the Technical Lead changes, say so in the channel. No silent handoffs.
  5. Escalate on a timer.No progress in 30 minutes on a Sev 1 or 60 minutes on a Sev 2? Escalate to the next tier or a secondary on-call.
Reality check: unclear owners

If you can't tell who owns a service (no cft.owner, a cryptic cloud_RoleName), don't let that stop you. Page Cloud Infrastructure as the default owner and keep mitigating. Then file an ownership gap as a follow-up.

Mitigate Before You Debug

Your first job is to reduce user impact, not to find the root cause. Most mitigations can be reversed, and an outage that runs on can't be undone. These are the levers you have:

LeverUse whenWhoRisk
Roll back service deployThe problem started after a deployCFTLow
Flip feature flag / revert prompt templateA prompt, model-version, or config change lines up with the startCFTLow
Halt client rolloutOnly a new desktop build is affectedClient CFTLow
Pause background workloads429s or saturation on a shared deploymentInsights CFT / Cloud InfrastructureLow (tips arrive late)
Shift traffic to another deployment or regionOne deployment or region is degradedCloud InfrastructureMedium (capacity, data residency)
Enable spillover / raise quotaSustained demand over PTU capacityCloud InfrastructureMedium (cost)
Graceful degradationA dependency such as retrieval is failingCFTMedium (lower-quality answers)
Scale out a serviceService CPU or thread saturationCFTLow

After the Incident

  • Write the postmortem draft within 2 business days (required for Sev 1–2).
  • Hold a blameless review with every involved CFT and Cloud Infrastructure.
  • Give every action item a single owner and a due date, and track it in the backlog.
  • File observability gaps you hit: missing dashboards, broken traces, misrouted alerts.
  • Update or create the playbook for this scenario.
  • Check that alerts would catch this sooner next time.

Anti-Patterns to Avoid

The Hero

One person debugs silently for an hour without declaring. Declare early, because it's cheap.

The Debugging Coordinator

The coordinator gets lost in a query and coordination stops. Coordinators coordinate.

The Deep-Diving Director

A leader pulls responders into explaining raw data. Name a comms buffer. See When Other Leaders Dive In.

Root Cause First

Users stay broken while you look for the "why". Mitigate first.

Parallel Incidents

Three CFTs open three channels for one Foundry problem. Merge them.

Write-Only Postmortems

Action items never get done. Track them like features.
