During an Incident

You don't need to read KQL to make an incident go better. Make sure the response is organized, make the decisions only you can make, and protect the people doing the work.

  • Ask four questions, once: Who is coordinating (the NOC IC)? What's the impact in numbers? What's the mitigation and next update? What do you need from me?
  • Your unique value is in decisions: spend, quality trade-offs, customer messaging, and pulling in people.
  • You are the top rung of the escalation ladder when CFTs don't respond.
  • Don't dive into the weeds, and help other leaders not to. See When a Leader Dives Too Deep.

Your Job in One Sentence

Make it easy for the people fixing the problem to fix it, and make the business decisions they can't make themselves.

The worst thing a senior leader can do in an incident is become another source of interruptions. The best thing is to remove obstacles: get the right team on the call, approve the costly mitigation quickly, and handle the executives so engineers don't have to.

During an Incident

The First Four Questions

Ask these in the incident channel, once. The answers tell you almost everything you need to know.

  1. "Who is coordinating?"At Nimbus the NOC is the Incident Coordinator, and an SRE is the Technical Lead. If either role is missing, getting it filled is the most important problem to solve.
  2. "What's the customer impact right now, in numbers?"For example: "About 12% of Device Care requests are failing." This tells you severity far better than technical detail does.
  3. "What's the current mitigation, and when is the next update?"This confirms the team is trying to stop the impact, not only investigating, and sets a rhythm so you don't need to ask again.
  4. "What do you need from me?"People, a decision, budget, or access to another team. Then deliver it quickly.

Avoid During an Incident

"What's the Root Cause?"

That's a question for afterwards. During the incident, ask what's being done to reduce impact.

Directing Engineers

Even good ideas from a senior leader take over the room. Send suggestions to the IC instead.

Side-Channel Pings

Messaging engineers directly pulls them away from the work. Read the incident channel, or ask the comms lead.

"Any Update?" Every 5 Minutes

Updates come on a schedule. If the schedule isn't working for you, ask the IC to change it, once.

A Big Audience on the Bridge

Ten observers make it harder to coordinate. Join, listen, and leave if you aren't needed.

When a Leader Dives Too Deep

Meet Morgan, the deep-diving director. Morgan (they/them) cares a lot and is under pressure from above. In incidents they ask for the raw queries, challenge each hypothesis, ask "why isn't this fixed yet?" every few minutes, DM engineers directly, and sometimes open a separate call with a CFT. Each move feels like help to Morgan. To the responders, it's interruptions and stress, and the incident gets longer.

Recognize It (Including in Yourself)

  • You're asking how something works rather than what you need to decide.
  • You're messaging responders directly instead of reading the channel.
  • You're asking for root cause while users are still affected.
  • You've started a second call or thread "just to get the real story".

Redirect a Peer or Director

  1. Be their comms buffer."I'll keep you updated every 15 minutes, so the team can stay heads-down."
  2. Give them something real to own.Escalating to another org's leadership, approving spend, or briefing executives above them.
  3. Ask privately, early."Your questions are good, but they're pulling the Technical Lead off the fix. Can we route them through me?"
  4. Back the coordinator.If the NOC redirects Morgan, support them in the channel. The coordinator's authority depends on leaders respecting it.

"Morgan, I'll be your direct line. You'll get an update from me every 15 minutes. Could you help by calling Cloud Infrastructure's director? That's the blocker right now."

Afterwards

Raise the pattern in the postmortem as a contributing factor in the system, never as a person: "There was no executive update format and no named comms buffer, so leaders went straight to responders." The usual action items are an exec update template, a comms buffer role, and a short briefing for directors on how incidents run.

Decisions Only You Can Make

These are where a senior manager adds the most value. When the team brings you one of these, decide quickly. A good decision now beats a perfect one in an hour.

Reading an Incident Update

A good update has five things. If one is missing, that's your question.

Status

Investigating, mitigating, monitoring, or resolved.

Impact

Who is affected and how badly, in numbers.

What Changed

What was tried since the last update, and what effect it had.

Next Step

What the team is doing now.

Next Update Time

A specific time, so you can stop checking.

Working With Cloud Infrastructure Leadership

Cloud Infrastructure owns the shared cloud and has a lot of influence. In incidents, your job is to keep the relationship workable and the evidence flowing:

  • Escalate peer-to-peer. If their on-call disputes the evidence, call their manager or director directly and calmly, with the Technical Lead's data.
  • Don't relitigate mid-incident. Disagreements about whose fault it is wait for the postmortem.
  • Agree what happens next time. Joint postmortem actions with their management in the room are how shared layers improve.

Our Reality: Slow CFTs and Two Chat Tools

  • At 30 minutes with no CFT response, SRE will tell you in the channel. At 45 minutes, contact that CFT's leadership directly. That's your rung of the escalation ladder.
  • The NOC runs the incident but can't change production. When they request an action from a CFT or Cloud Infrastructure and it stalls, you're the one who can unblock it.
  • Read the Slack incident channel. It's the source of truth. Teams-only CFTs get updates through a bridge person, so don't start a separate Teams thread with them about the incident.
  • Don't ask for a Jira ticket mid-incident. Jira is locked down, so the record is kept in Slack and filed in Jira afterwards.
