Senior Manager · Live Operations

During an Incident

In a live incident, your job is to make sure the response is organized, to ask the few questions that sharpen it, to make the decisions only you can make, and to keep executives informed so they don't reach into the room.

  • First check that a coordinator (NOC) and a Technical Lead (SRE) are in place.
  • Then run LAYERS in live mode: Locate, Assess, Yesterday, Evidence, Response. Ask once, in the channel, at update times.
  • Your unique value is decisions (spend, trade-offs, customer messaging, people) and unblocking stalled requests and silent teams.
  • Send executives a SITREP before they ask (builder).

Your Job in One Sentence

Make it easy for the people fixing the problem to fix it, make the decisions they can't, and keep everyone above you informed and out of the way.

LAYERS in Live Mode

Ask these in the incident channel, through the coordinator, at update times. Each answer you get (or don't get) tells you where the response is strong or stuck.

StepAskA good answer sounds like
0 · Roles"Who's coordinating, and who's the Technical Lead?""NOC (Priya) is IC. SRE (Jordan) is Tech Lead."
L · Locate"Which layer is it in, and how do we know?""The model provider layer: 429s on chat-small, and the gateway and edge are normal."
A · Assess"How many users, compared to normal? P50 or P95?""9% of Device Care requests failing, normally under 1%, for 25 minutes."
Y · Yesterday"What changed before it started?""The Proactive Tips batch started at 09:00, and a local model rollout reached 25% at 08:30."
E · Evidence"How confident are you, and what would prove us wrong?""Medium. Tokens by caller shows the batch. Confirming the fallback rate next."
R · Response"What's the safest reversible action, who owns it, and what do you need from me?""We've asked Insights to pause the batch. If it's not enough, we need approval for overflow capacity at about $900/h."

Keep S · Sustain for afterwards. Note ideas, but don't push prevention work while users are still affected. See Post-Incident Actions.

Decisions Only You Can Make

When the team brings you one of these, decide quickly. A good decision now beats a perfect one in an hour.

DecisionTypical exampleAsk before deciding
Spend money to restore serviceOverflow (pay-as-you-go) model capacity"What does it cost per hour, and what does it fix?"
Trade quality for availabilityA smaller model, or no retrieval, temporarily"How much worse are answers, and for how long?"
Choose which feature to protectPause background tips so Device Care works"Which users notice each option?"
Disable a partner integrationTurn off the Zoom connector during their outage"What do those users see instead? Who tells the partner?"
Move traffic to another regionFail over the model deployment"Any data-residency or contract implications?"
Halt a releaseStop a desktop app or local model rollout"What's the launch or partner impact of pausing?"
Tell customers or the publicStatus page, in-app banner, support messaging"What do we know for certain, and when is the next update?"
Pull people off other workAn expert from another CFT"Who exactly, and for how long?"

Unblocking

  • Silent CFTs: at 30 minutes the coordinator tells you. At 45 minutes, contact that CFT's leadership yourself (ladder).
  • Stalled NOC requests: the NOC can't change production. When an owner sits on a request, you're the one who can get it actioned.
  • Cloud Infrastructure disputes: escalate peer to peer, calmly, with the Technical Lead's evidence. Don't argue about fault mid-incident.
  • Partners: if the partner's support channel is slow, use the executive or account contact. Make sure someone owns the partner relationship during the incident.

Reading an Incident Update

A good update has five parts. If one is missing, that's your question.

Status

Investigating, mitigating, monitoring, or resolved.

Impact

Who, how badly, in numbers, against a baseline.

What Changed

What was tried since the last update, and the effect.

Next Step

What the team is doing now, and who.

Next Update

A specific time, so you can stop checking.

Avoid During an Incident

"What's the Root Cause?"

That's for afterwards. Ask what's being done to reduce impact.

Directing Engineers

Even good ideas from a leader take over the room. Route them through the coordinator.

Direct Messages

DMs pull responders off the work. Read the channel.

Side Calls

A second bridge splits decisions. Keep one channel.

Diving Into the Weeds

If you're asking how something works rather than what you need to decide, step back. Engineers can explain the details later, in the postmortem.

When Other Leaders Dive In

Directors under pressure from above sometimes ask for raw queries, message engineers, or start side calls. At Nimbus, that's Morgan, in our background story. Your job is to be the comms buffer:

  1. Get ahead with a SITREP.Updates on a schedule remove most of the reasons to dive in.
  2. Offer a direct line."I'll update you every 30 minutes, so the team can stay heads-down."
  3. Give them a real job.A decision, or an escalation to another org's leadership or a partner's executive.
  4. Back the coordinator.If the NOC redirects a leader, support them publicly.
  5. Raise it afterwards as a system gap."No exec update format, no comms buffer." Never name a person.

Our Reality

  • The Slack incident channel is the source of truth. Teams-only CFTs are reached through a bridge person.
  • Jira is locked down. The record lives in Slack and is filed in Jira afterwards. Don't ask for a ticket mid-incident.
  • The NOC is offshore and still building technical depth. Keep your questions to them in plain English, one at a time (how).
