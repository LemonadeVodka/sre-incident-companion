Latency & Errors Primer

Two signals cover most of what goes wrong in an AI product: how long users wait (latency) and how often requests fail (error rate). Learn to read them well and you can ask sharp questions about almost any incident. Plain language first. Open the "Go Deeper" sections if you want the detail.

  • P50 is the typical wait. P95 is the bad-day wait, what 1 in 20 users experiences. Never accept an average.
  • For AI, time to first token (when the answer starts appearing) matters more than total time.
  • An error code tells you which layer to look at: 401 means sign-in, 403 the firewall, 429 capacity or quota, 5xx something broke, and a timeout means something was too slow.
  • Every number needs a baseline. "Compared to what?" is your most powerful question.

Latency

Percentiles, Not Averages

P50 (Median)

Half of requests are faster than this. The typical experience.

P95

95% are faster, so 1 in 20 users waits longer. The bad-day experience. Most complaints live here.

Average

Misleading. A few very slow requests hide behind a calm-looking average. Ask for P50 and P95 instead.

AI answers stream in word by word. Users judge speed by how long until the first words appear, not by when the last word arrives.

The Latency Budget

Every layer spends part of the user's patience. In a typical Nimbus Writing Assist request at P95 (about 6 seconds), the model takes roughly 70%, retrieval about 10%, and everything else shares the rest. When P95 grows, ask: "Which layer's share grew?" The engineers can show this with a per-hop breakdown (example).

Why AI Latency Behaves Differently
  • Input tokens (prompt size) slow the start. Output tokens (answer length) slow the end.
  • Reserved capacity (PTU) is fast until it's full. Then requests queue, spill to slower overflow capacity, or get 429.
  • Model version changes can change answer length and speed without any code change.
  • Retries hide errors but add latency. A rise in "calls per request" is a warning sign.
  • On-device answers are fast, but if they fail and fall back to the cloud, cloud latency rises for everyone.

Error Rates

Error rate is the share of requests that fail, measured from the user's point of view. Make sure the team is counting failures users actually see, not retries that succeeded.

401"I don't accept your credentials"Gateway/auth, a partner (expired OAuth), or the model (our identity)"Everyone, one app version, or one partner? Did a key or setting change or expire?"
403"I know who you are, but no"Edge firewall (WAF), or permissions"Did a security rule change?"

Quiet Isn't Always Healthy

If requests fail before reaching our services (device, edge, sign-in), our service error rate can look perfect while users can't connect. When users complain and charts are green, ask: "Could the failure be before our telemetry can see it?"

Error Budgets and Burn Rate

An SLO is a target, for example "99.5% of requests succeed over 28 days". The error budget is the 0.5% allowed to fail. Burn rate is how fast that budget is being used: a burn rate of 14× means a month's budget would be gone in about two days. Alerts based on burn rate catch real user impact and ignore blips. See SRE Fundamentals.

Numbers That Should Worry You

  • Error rate above 2× its baseline for more than 10 minutes.
  • P95 above 1.5× the same hour yesterday for more than 10 minutes.
  • Any 429s on a model deployment that serves customer-facing features.
  • A 401 rate step-change, which often means a configuration change or an expiry.
  • Telemetry volume dropping, which means we may be blind.
  • Cloud traffic rising with no server-side change, which may be a device fallback surge.
  • Errors for one partner's users only. Check the partner's status first.
