After & between incidents

What happens after an incident decides whether you have it again. Between incidents, a few good questions each month tell you more than any dashboard.

  • Thank responders, keep postmortems blameless, and fund the action items.
  • As a new team, the most valuable follow-up is often a runbook. Make sure one gets written.
  • Each month, review SLO health, detection source, CFT response times, runbook coverage, and action-item completion.

After the incident

  • Thank the responders, by name and in public. Incidents are stressful, and recognition makes people willing to take on-call.
  • Attend the postmortem review for Sev 1–2. Listen more than you talk.
  • Protect blamelessness. If anyone asks "who did this?", redirect to "how did the system allow this?". People hide information when they fear blame, and hidden information causes the next outage.
  • Fund the action items. Make sure each one has an owner, a date, and room in the roadmap. Action items that never get done mean you'll have the same incident again.
  • Check a runbook was assigned. We're a new team, so every incident should leave one behind.
  • Follow up in 30 days. Ask: "Which action items are done, and which are stuck?"

Good postmortem questions

  • "How did we find out? Would we have found out without a customer telling us?"
  • "What made this harder to diagnose than it should have been?"
  • "Where did we lose time? Was it detection, finding the owner, or deciding?"
  • "What would have to be true for this to never happen again, and what's a realistic step toward that?"
  • "Did anyone feel they couldn't escalate or speak up?"

Between incidents: monthly health check

Ask these in your monthly operations review. You'll learn more than any status dashboard can show you.

QuestionWhy it matters
"Are we meeting our SLOs for the top features? How much error budget is left?"This is the clearest sign of reliability from the user's point of view.
"How were our incidents detected: alerts or customers?"Customer-detected incidents mean we can't see our own product.
"How long from the page to knowing who owns the problem?"In an immature org, unclear ownership is often the biggest delay.
"How many pages did on-call get, and how many needed action?"Noisy alerts burn people out and train them to ignore pages.
"How long did CFTs take to acknowledge pages this month? Which CFTs still have no PagerDuty service?"Slow CFT response is one of our biggest known delays. These numbers come from pinned incident records.
"How many runbooks did we write this month? How many of last month's incidents had one?"We're a new team. Runbook coverage is how we stop solving the same incident from scratch.
"What share of postmortem action items are done?"This shows whether you're learning or just writing documents.
"What share of our features have a dashboard and an SLO?"Today it's under 10%. Track it until it's 100% for interactive features.
"Where do teams share capacity, and who decides priority when it runs out?"Noisy-neighbor incidents are a governance problem as much as a technical one.

Red flags

Act when you see these
  • Incidents keep happening with no Incident Commander, or with two parallel channels.
  • The same incident repeats because the action items weren't done.
  • One engineer is the only person who can diagnose a feature.
  • On-call engineers are afraid to escalate or to declare an incident.
  • Alerts go to channels that nobody owns.
  • Teams argue about whose problem it is while users are affected.
