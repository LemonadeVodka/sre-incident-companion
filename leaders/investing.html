Investing in reliability

Incidents show where we're weak. This page turns that into decisions: what reliability targets to set, what to fund first, and which tooling and process fixes only leadership can unlock.

  • Reliability targets are business decisions. Set them with product, per feature, and back them when they bite.
  • Fund in this order: people and process (on-call, runbooks), then visibility (dashboards, alerts), then targets (SLOs), then resilience (capacity, failover).
  • Five tooling asks only leadership can unlock: a Jira incident project, a Teams workflow, PagerDuty for every CFT, CFT response expectations, and dedicated observability capacity.

Reliability targets are business decisions

An SLO (service level objective) is a reliability target, such as "99.5% of Writing Assist requests succeed" or "95% of Smart Search results arrive within 3.5 seconds". Every extra "9" costs more engineering time and money, so targets should differ by feature:

FeatureWho notices failureSuggested starting target
Writing Assist, Smart SearchUsers immediately, many times a day99.5% success, P95 within baseline
Device CareUsers with a problem, at a frustrating moment99% task success
Meeting RecapUsers after meetings. A short delay is tolerable.99% delivered within 5 minutes
Proactive TipsBarely, because it runs in the background98% delivered within 15 minutes

The error budget is the failure the target allows. Agree up front what happens when it runs out. Typically, that team pauses risky launches and works on reliability. If leadership won't honor that agreement, the targets mean nothing.

What to fund first

  1. People and process.An on-call rotation and a PagerDuty service for every CFT. Time for runbooks after every incident. These are cheap and have an immediate effect on our slowest delays.
  2. Visibility.Standard monitoring for every service, and one dashboard template applied to every feature. This takes us from under 10% coverage to all customer-facing features in about 90 days.
  3. Targets.SLOs for the top two features, and alerts based on them instead of noisy thresholds.
  4. Resilience.Separate AI capacity for customer-facing and background work, tested failover, and capacity planning for every launch.

The engineering detail is on the observability maturity page.

The business case

Shorter outages

Most incident time is spent finding the problem and the owner, not fixing it. Better visibility and on-call coverage cut that directly.

Fewer repeat incidents

Runbooks and completed action items stop the same incident from happening again.

Lower AI spend

Knowing who uses capacity, and when, avoids over-buying reserved capacity and paying for emergency overflow.

Less burnout

Fewer noisy pages and a shared load mean people stay on the team. Losing one of our few experts is a reliability risk in itself.

Tooling and process asks

These are blocked by permissions, budgets, or other teams, which makes them leadership work.

Questions for planning reviews

  • "Does this launch change how much AI capacity we use? Who checked?"
  • "Does this new feature have monitoring, an owner on PagerDuty, and a runbook before launch?"
  • "Which reliability action items from last quarter are still open, and why?"
  • "What would it cost to separate customer-facing AI capacity from background work?"
  • "When did we last test switching to the backup region?"
