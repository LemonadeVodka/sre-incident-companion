For senior managers

You don't need to read KQL or know what a PTU is to make an incident go better. Your job is to make sure the response is organized, to make the decisions only you can make, and to protect the team doing the work. This page tells you what to do and what to ask.

  • During an incident: check there's an Incident Commander, ask about customer impact, offer help, and then step back.
  • Your unique value is in decisions: spending money, trading quality for availability, telling customers, and pulling people off other work.
  • After the incident: keep the postmortem blameless and make sure the action items actually get funded and done.

Your job in one sentence

Make it easy for the people fixing the problem to fix it, and make the business decisions they can't make themselves.

The worst thing a senior leader can do in an incident is become another source of interruptions. The best thing is to remove obstacles: get the right team on the call, approve the costly mitigation quickly, and handle the executives so engineers don't have to.

During an incident

The first four questions

Ask these in the incident channel, once. The answers tell you almost everything you need to know.

  1. "Who is the Incident Commander?"If nobody is, that's the most important problem to solve, and you can ask someone to take the role. Don't take it yourself unless you're trained for it.
  2. "What's the customer impact right now, in numbers?"For example: "About 12% of Device Care requests are failing." This tells you severity far better than technical detail does.
  3. "What's the current mitigation, and when is the next update?"This confirms the team is trying to stop the impact, not only investigating, and sets a rhythm so you don't need to ask again.
  4. "What do you need from me?"People, a decision, budget, or access to another team. Then deliver it quickly.

Questions by phase

PhaseGood questionsWhat a good answer sounds like
TriageIs it confirmed? Which feature? How many users? What severity?"Confirmed. Writing Assist only, about 20% of requests, Sev 2."
MitigationWhat are we trying first? What's the risk? How will we know it worked?"Rolling back yesterday's deploy. Low risk. We'll see the error rate drop within 10 minutes."
EscalationIs it ours or the shared platform's? Is the right team on the call? Do we need Microsoft support?"It's on the shared model deployment. Platform has joined, and an Azure case is open at Sev A."
ResolutionIs the impact gone, or just lower? How long have we been stable?"Back to baseline for 30 minutes. Resolving now."
Wrap-upWho writes the postmortem? When is the review?"The IC drafts it by Thursday. The review is Friday with Platform and the Create CFT."

Avoid during an incident

"What's the root cause?"

That's a question for afterwards. During the incident, ask what's being done to reduce impact.

Directing engineers

Even good ideas from a senior leader take over the room. Send suggestions to the IC instead.

Side-channel pings

Messaging engineers directly pulls them away from the work. Read the incident channel, or ask the comms lead.

"Any update?" every 5 minutes

Updates come on a schedule. If the schedule isn't working for you, ask the IC to change it, once.

A big audience on the bridge

Ten observers make it harder to coordinate. Join, listen, and leave if you aren't needed.

Decisions only you can make

These are where a senior manager adds the most value. When the team brings you one of these, decide quickly. A good decision now beats a perfect one in an hour.

DecisionTypical Nimbus exampleAsk before deciding
Spend money to restore serviceTurn on pay-as-you-go spillover or buy extra model capacity"What does it cost per hour, and what does it fix?"
Trade quality for availabilitySwitch to a smaller model or turn off retrieval temporarily"How much worse are answers, and for how long?"
Choose which feature to protectPause Proactive Tips so Device Care works"Which users notice each option?"
Move traffic to another regionFail over from East US 2 to Sweden Central"Are there data-residency or contract implications?"
Halt a releaseStop the rollout of a new desktop build"What's the partner or launch impact of pausing?"
Tell customers or the publicStatus page, support macros, partner notification"What do we know for certain, and when is the next update?"
Pull people off other workBring in an expert from another CFT"Who exactly, and for how long?"

Reading an incident update

A good update has five things. If one is missing, that's your question.

Status

Investigating, mitigating, monitoring, or resolved.

Impact

Who is affected and how badly, in numbers.

What changed

What was tried since the last update, and what effect it had.

Next step

What the team is doing now.

Next update time

A specific time, so you can stop checking.

After the incident

  • Thank the responders, by name and in public. Incidents are stressful, and recognition makes people willing to take on-call.
  • Attend the postmortem review for Sev 1–2. Listen more than you talk.
  • Protect blamelessness. If anyone asks "who did this?", redirect to "how did the system allow this?". People hide information when they fear blame, and hidden information causes the next outage.
  • Fund the action items. Make sure each one has an owner, a date, and room in the roadmap. Action items that never get done mean you'll have the same incident again.
  • Follow up in 30 days. Ask: "Which action items are done, and which are stuck?"

Good postmortem questions

  • "How did we find out? Would we have found out without a customer telling us?"
  • "What made this harder to diagnose than it should have been?"
  • "Where did we lose time? Was it detection, finding the owner, or deciding?"
  • "What would have to be true for this to never happen again, and what's a realistic step toward that?"
  • "Did anyone feel they couldn't escalate or speak up?"

Between incidents: monthly health check

Ask these in your monthly operations review. You'll learn more than any status dashboard can show you.

QuestionWhy it matters
"Are we meeting our SLOs for the top features? How much error budget is left?"This is the clearest sign of reliability from the user's point of view.
"How were our incidents detected: alerts or customers?"Customer-detected incidents mean we can't see our own product.
"How long from the page to knowing who owns the problem?"In an immature org, unclear ownership is often the biggest delay.
"How many pages did on-call get, and how many needed action?"Noisy alerts burn people out and train them to ignore pages.
"What share of postmortem action items are done?"This shows whether you're learning or just writing documents.
"What share of our features have a dashboard and an SLO?"Today it's under 10%. Track it until it's 100% for interactive features.
"Where do teams share capacity, and who decides priority when it runs out?"Noisy-neighbor incidents are a governance problem as much as a technical one.

Red flags

Act when you see these
  • Incidents keep happening with no Incident Commander, or with two parallel channels.
  • The same incident repeats because the action items weren't done.
  • One engineer is the only person who can diagnose a feature.
  • On-call engineers are afraid to escalate or to declare an incident.
  • Alerts go to channels that nobody owns.
  • Teams argue about whose problem it is while users are affected.
