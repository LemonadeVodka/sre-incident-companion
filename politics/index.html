Stakeholders & Politics Playbook

Reliability work is political by nature. You depend on teams you don't manage, a director with strong opinions, vendors with their own incentives, and executives who mostly hear from you when something breaks. Politics here doesn't mean manipulation. It means getting the right things done through people you don't control, without burning trust.

  • Political capital is earned with reliability, credibility, and favors, and spent on asks. Track it.
  • Map stakeholders by power and interest, and know what each one needs to "win".
  • Pre-wire every important decision. No surprises in meetings.
  • Manage up deliberately, especially with a highly technical, low-EQ leader.
  • Escalate cleanly, and own the narrative after incidents with facts, early.

Principles

Be Useful Before You Ask

Help other teams first: data for their reviews, quick incident support, credit in your reports. Asks land better when you've already given.

No Surprises

Leaders hate surprises more than bad news. Tell them early, privately, with a plan.

Data Over Opinion

Especially with technical leaders and powerful teams. A chart beats an argument.

Make Others Look Good

Credit generously and in public. Take blame for the system, never for an individual.

Disagree, Then Commit

Argue your case privately and well. Once a decision is made, support it publicly, or escalate properly.

Protect Your Team

Absorb pressure, pass down actions, and never let politics land on an individual engineer.

Political Capital

Think of trust as a balance. You earn it by delivering and helping, and you spend it on asks, escalations, and pushback.

Earns capitalSpends capital
Reliable delivery, and incidents handled calmlyAsking for budget, headcount, or tooling
Clear, honest, early updatesEscalating over someone's head
Helping other teams with data or supportPushing back on a director's or executive's decision
Crediting others publiclyAsking teams to change priorities for reliability
Predictions that came true ("I flagged this risk")Being wrong loudly

Keep a short ledger: what you've delivered, who you've helped, and what you plan to ask for. Spend capital on things that matter, such as NOC tooling or isolating capacity, not on small wins.
